# Methods and stories from similar climates

These are not week-long irrigation ponds. They are **ephemeral-wadi / dryland** structures whose first job is to slow floods and trap sediment. Water stands for **hours to a few days**, then the bed is dry. Climate is in the same family as southern Afghanistan: 150–400 mm rain, flash floods, alluvial fans, karez/qanat in some places.

Use them as **stories and methods**, not as promised numbers for Kandahar–Zabul. Copy the *way they measured*, and the *honest lesson* (silt, local mound, control catchment).

---

## 1. Pakistan — Balochistan delay-action dams and leaky dams (closest analogue)

**Story.** Balochistan is the neighbour with the same karez culture, desert heat, and flash floods. The province built more than **160 delay-action dams** so floodwater would sit and soak. Fine clay sealed many reservoir beds. The dams became **evaporation ponds**; karezes around Quetta kept drying as tubewells proliferated. At **Margat**, about 40 km from Quetta, a **leaky dam** was tried: water is released slowly into the wadi instead of being held as an open lake. After rain, wells rose **about 1–3 feet (0.3–0.9 m)**. An ADB pilot later put a leaky structure upstream of Dargai dam and recharge wells/galleries at Murghi Kotal and Dargai, aiming to restore karez flow and open-well levels — the same livelihood story as Kandahar karez.

**Method.** Compare delay-action (tight wall, silted bed) vs leaky release; measure well and karez levels immediately after floods; work with villagers on maintenance; treat silt as the failure mode.

**Copy.** Your dams are closer to **leaky / delay-action** than to Indian monsoon tanks. Measure **karez + wells after each flood**. Do not assume a silted basin is recharging.

*ADB PDA “Groundwater aquifer rejuvenation, Balochistan”; ReliefWeb / PCRWR notes on Margat leaky dam; Kahlown & Abdullah (2004) on gabions.*

## 2. Tunisia — Jessour, tabias and gabions (~200 mm rain)

**Story.** In the Matmata mountains and Zeuss–Koutine watershed, families have built **jessour** for centuries: a stone or earth dyke 2–5 m high across a steep wadi, a terrace of olives/almonds/figs behind it, and a spillway so a cascade of jessour overflows from one to the next. Water stands as a **temporary pond**, soaks the terrace, and is said to feed a “small aquifer in each jesr.” **Tabias** on the piedmont do the same on gentler fans. Modern **gabion check dams** were added for flood control. SWAT-WH on Wadi Koutine (209 mm/year over 12 years) split rainfall roughly **72% ET, 22% recharge, 6% outflow**. Gabion basins later filled with silt; ERT surveys showed the deposit, and teams recommended **cleaning the floor** or the structure stops recharging.

**Method.** Event runoff at a downstream station (38 floods, 1973–1985); treat each jessr as catchment–dyke–terrace; soil moisture up- and downstream; sediment mapping behind gabions.

**Copy.** Cascade + spillway + temporary pond on a **200 mm** wadi is your climate. Measure soil/terrace moisture *and* wells; budget silt cleaning.

*Ouessar et al. 2009, HESS; Ouessar PhD / WUR; Water 15(1361) on upstream–downstream jessour moisture.*

## 3. Ethiopia — Tigray gullies: floods drop, wells appear

**Story.** In the Tigray highlands, gullies were eating fields. Communities (with Mekelle University / Nyssen and others) built hundreds of **dry-stone and gabion check dams**, stone bunds, and exclosures (no grazing). In one **200 ha** catchment with **242 check dams**, measured outlet runoff in 2006 was only **5 mm (1.6% of rain)** versus an estimated **26.5 mm (8%)** before treatment — about an **81% cut in direct runoff**. The water table rose faster after the rains; **new wells and small irrigation** appeared; farmers started cropping **inside former gully floors** because they were less afraid of flash floods. On instrumented 50 m gully reaches, check dams **plus vegetation** delayed the peak by **~60%** and cut runoff volume **8–18%** along that short reach, versus **4–6%** in untreated gullies. A later drought-resilience study (Gule watershed) still used gabion checks as flood and percolation structures, with community workshops after the 2015/16 drought.

**Method.** Control vs treated gully; **upper and lower** water-level sensors (or V-notch) on the same reach; catchment outlet runoff; water-table monitoring; farmer interviews (wells, irrigation, fear of floods).

**Copy.** This is the **flood-first success story**. Put a gauge above and below your dam. Count new or recovering wells as a livelihood indicator, not only metres of DTW.

*Nyssen et al. 2010, Hydrol. Process.; Guyassa et al. 2017, J. Hydrol.; ISWCR 2023 Gule watershed.*

## 4. USA — Arizona leaky weirs (Cienega Ranch)

**Story.** A rancher in south-east Arizona (Dos Cabezas mountain front, same kind of fan as Kandahar) wanted wet meadows (*ciénegas*) back for cattle and wildlife. From 2014 he built **15 leaky weirs**: rock walls cemented to bedrock **designed to leak**, not to hold a lake. Researchers (USGS / university partners) instrumented two weirs and **three adjacent watersheds** for **16 months**. Peaks dropped by about **70%** at one weir. Two weirs were estimated to trap **~2,700 tons** of sand. Isotopes showed the **deep** basin groundwater was still old mountain-block water — the weirs were **not** refilling the regional aquifer. They **were** building **shallow alluvial pockets** that stay wetter and support wetlands. During the growing season those pockets feed plants; in the off-season they recharge the shallow table.

**Method.** 10-minute loggers, wildlife cameras on gauges, crest-stage + soil temperature, well/spring isotopes, **treated vs adjacent untreated outlets**, one flood dissected in full water-budget detail.

**Copy.** Honest split: **flood and shallow wadi gravel = yes; deep aquifer = maybe not.** Your I4 should be near-wadi wells and karez, not a provincial DACAAR well 10 km away.

*Norman et al. 2025, Appl. Water Sci.; USGS pub. 70263441.*

## 5. Oman — “Dry” / recharge dams that empty between floods

**Story.** Oman built dozens of **recharge dams** on wadis so flash floods would soak alluvial aquifers instead of running to the sea (and to blunt coastal saltwater intrusion). They are **not lakes**: they fill in a flood and are meant to empty. **Al-Khoud** (Muscat), commissioned 1985, ~11.6 million m³, 5.1 km long. After ~30 years, **3.4 million m³ of silt** sat in the basin; storage fell **>30%**; infiltration dropped by about **ten times** (field tests: order of **16 cm/h down to 0.18 cm/h** in related “dry dam” work). Water then skimmed downstream as muddy overflow and clogged the recharge basin. The government response: scrape silt, or build **small upstream checks** as sediment traps because small basins are cheaper to clean. Gauging sticks and satellite maps are now used to see silt from space.

**Method.** Repeat infiltrometer / K tests on the dry bed; topographic silt surveys; storage-capacity resurvey; some automatic gauges.

**Copy.** Hours of storage **will shrink every year** unless silt is measured and removed. Put Form D (sediment) in year 1, not as an afterthought.

*Al-Maktoum / Al-Saqri / Prathapar papers; DPRI Kyoto overview; Sci. Rep. silt mapping 2020.*

## 6. Spain — Rambla check dams (SE semi-arid)

**Story.** South-east Spain (Almería, Murcia, Almanzora and similar *ramblas*) built thousands of small check dams after the 1970s for **erosion and torrents**, not for irrigation tanks. Many sit dry most of the year. Martín-Rosales and colleagues looked at **67 dams**: they used **infiltrometer tests** on the permeable bed and a hydrologic model of inflow. Recharge was estimated at **6–53% of streamflow**, higher where storage was larger. A later national GIS study of ~27,680 transverse structures called this **“unintentional recharge”**: Spain did not design them as MAR, but slowing water still soaks aquifers — with a clogging correction because silt is everywhere.

**Method.** Infiltrometer + catchment model when you cannot sit through every flood; inventory geometry × permeability × groundwater depth; clogging factor.

**Copy.** If staff miss the night flood, **infiltrometer × estimated hours wet × area** is an accepted Mediterranean method. Report a **range**, not one number (Cyprus later showed how wide the uncertainty is).

*Martín-Rosales et al. 2007; Environments 2023 “Unintentional recharge… Spain”.*

## 7. Cyprus — Peristerona ephemeral river

**Story.** A 25,000 m³ check dam on a semi-arid island river. Over four years it took about **10.4 million m³/year** of streamflow and an estimated **3.1 million m³/year** went to groundwater (~**30%**), more than an **11 km untreated** channel upstream (~1.5 million m³/year). Authors stressed **huge uncertainty** (0.1 to 9.6 million m³/year) because inflow and bed K are hard in flash floods. Sediment build-up was measured at the same time.

**Method.** Survey dam topography; stage–volume; inflow/outflow when possible; water balance **at the dam and on a natural reach**; publish uncertainty.

**Copy.** Always compare **treated reach vs untreated reach**. Do not only photograph the dam.

*Djuma et al. 2017, Water 9:813.*

## 8. Morocco — Pre-Saharan flood basins (farm MAR)

**Story.** In traditional oases, floods were spread on fields. Some farmers now **earthen basins** that fill in a flood then empty by infiltration (sometimes with a recharge well). CIRAD monitored one basin for **7 months / 3 floods** with pressure sensors in the basin, the well, and nearby boreholes. Infiltration recharged groundwater **locally, within about 360 m**. A farther piezometer did not respond. After each flood, **silt cut the infiltration rate**. Natural wadi-bed recharge next to the basin was weak because the bed was already clogged.

**Method.** Event loggers; height–area–volume of the basin; piezometers at several distances; repeat after each flood.

**Copy.** Your benefited wells will likely sit **within a few hundred metres**. Put W-N / W-M / W-F rings on the map and do not promise a whole district.

*CIRAD / Agritrop farm-scale MAR, pre-Saharan Morocco.*

## 9. Yemen — Sana’a cascade of low dams

**Story.** Sana’a Basin is data-poor and arid — a planning cousin of Afghanistan. Alderwish compared one **gravity dam** with a **cascade of three 2–4 m check dams** on a small wadi. The cascade was predicted to recharge **~36% more** (~141,000 vs ~104,000 m³/year) without heavy foundations. Spreadsheet water balance and a simple Darcy estimate gave similar answers; **hydraulic conductivity of the bed** dominated the result.

**Method.** Simple water balance vs Darcy; scenario of cascade vs one high wall.

**Copy.** Several small flood dams on one wadi can beat one high dam for **both** peak cutting and soak time — but then you must monitor the **chain** (upstream dams starve downstream ones).

*Alderwish 2010, Arab. J. Geosci.*

## 10. India — Rajasthan / Gujarat (contrast, not a copy of the pond)

**Story.** Hard-rock, semi-arid, but **monsoon ponds that last days to weeks**. MARVI trained village *Bhujal Jankars* to read gauges daily. Four check dams near Udaipur recharged on the order of **one to two times their storage per year**; infiltration was **5–8 × evaporation**; farmer photos made the data trustworthy. Manual desilting **raised** recharge (~84%); machine scraping **lowered** it (compaction). Gujarat modelling showed that if the catchment is large, extra rain is **overflow**, not extra soak.

**Method.** Daily stage + rain; dry-day water balance; control wells; photo QA.

**Copy.** Steal **observer QA, control wells, manual desilt**. Do **not** steal daily empty-pond MDWIR as your main KPI if the pond is gone by morning.

*Dashora et al. 2018, 2019; Jadeja / MARVI; Frontiers in Water 2021 Gujarat tool.*

## 11. China — Loess Plateau sediment dams

**Story.** More than **100,000** check dams, built first to stop yellow silt, not to make lakes. Behind the wall a **dam-land** farm forms. At one instrumented dam, the water table started rising **about 35 days** after the first big rain — much faster than the deep loess tablelands (decades). Preferential flow carried ~36% of recharge. A dense silt layer later **blocks** infiltration (lab and field infiltration curves).

**Method.** Water-table time series; isotopes; infiltration tests through the silt layer.

**Copy.** Read wells not only at +1 / +3 / +7 days but again at **~30 days**. Silt is both the product and the enemy.

*Huang et al. 2013, J. Hydrol. Eng.; Shi et al. 2021, J. Hydrol.; Water 14:1814 infiltration paper.*

## 12. Kenya — Sand dams (related, but different physics)

**Story.** In Kitui / Machakos / Makueni, communities (Africa Sand Dam Foundation, Excellent Development) build a wall on a **sandy seasonal river**. The point is **not** an open pond: floods dump **coarse sand**, and water is stored **in the sand** (low evaporation). Families scoop or use a shallow well 3–10 m upstream. One dam typically keeps only **1–3%** of river flow; the rest passes. Check dams that **fill completely with sediment** become, in effect, sand dams (China’s filled checks are in that family).

**Method.** Sand grain size (must be coarse); wells in the sand body; community labour; not a stage-decline pond balance.

**Copy.** Only if your wadi bed is **coarse sand** and you **want** the basin to fill with sand. That is a design choice. If your dam is masonry/gabion meant to stay an empty stilling basin, do not call it a sand dam.

*Maddrell & Neal 2012 practical guide; ASDF / Excellent Development.*

## What these stories agree on (for your presentation)

1. **Flood first, groundwater second.** Tigray and Arizona report peak cuts and then local water. Balochistan and Oman show that if you only chase recharge and ignore silt, you get a warm puddle.
2. **The water that people feel is shallow and close** — karez, dug wells, gully-floor fields, wetlands — usually **hundreds of metres**, not a whole basin (Morocco ~360 m; Arizona shallow alluvium vs deep aquifer).
3. **Hours of detention still count** if the bed is gravel and you **measure the event** (logger, camera, or Form E). Spain and Cyprus did not need a permanent lake.
4. **A control catchment is the difference between a story and evidence** (Tigray treated vs untreated gully; Arizona three watersheds; Cyprus dam vs natural reach).
5. **Silt is the plot twist** in every arid country (Oman, Tunisia, Balochistan, China, Morocco). Assessment without a silt grid will age badly.
6. **Community observers work** if you train them for **floods**, not for a daily pond (MARVI photos; Tigray farmer wells; Balochistan village meetings).

**One sentence for a donor slide:**  
*In climates like ours, leaky or short-detention check dams have repeatedly cut flash-flood peaks and wet the wadi gravel enough to lift nearby wells and karez; they have not, by themselves, restored regional aquifers — and they stop soaking when silt seals the bed.*

FAO **WaPOR** is sometimes cited as a groundwater tool. It is ET and biomass at 300 m over Afghanistan. Published WaPOR “recharge” maps (IGwA, WA+) are monthly soil-column or basin residuals, not check-dam mounds. Use WaPOR only as basin P/ET context; keep I4/I5 as the proof.
